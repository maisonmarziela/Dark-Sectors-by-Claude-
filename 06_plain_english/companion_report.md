# Could another world share our space and touch us?

**A plain-English account of Project CRH**

*No physics background is assumed. Technical words are explained when they first appear, and there is a short glossary at the end. This report describes a theoretical study carried out by an AI system; nobody has checked it independently.*

---

## The question

Imagine a second world that occupies the very same space as ours — its own matter, its own planets, its own people — but which we cannot see, hear or bump into, and which cannot see us. Now imagine that, just once, for a split second, someone in that world reaches out a hand in the same spot where you are standing, and you *feel* it. Then nothing.

Is there any way to write down laws of physics in which that could happen, without breaking the rules that physics has tested very thoroughly — that energy and momentum are never created from nothing, that nothing outruns light, that empty space is stable?

The project did not assume the answer was yes.

## The answer in one paragraph

Half of the idea is already ordinary physics: invisible "hidden worlds" sharing our space have been studied for sixty years, and nothing forbids them. The other half — the brief touch — can be made to work *on paper*, and we built a mathematical model in which it does. But to make the touch strong enough to feel, the model needs an unknown force about twenty thousand times stronger than any that the known ways of linking hidden worlds to ours can provide; it needs that force to be hidden by a switch that is either absurdly finely balanced or not really a switch; and it runs into several other problems we could not solve. **Our verdict: not strictly impossible, extremely unlikely, and supported by no evidence whatsoever.** The most solid things we found are reasons why it is hard.

---

## Step by step

### 1. "In the same place" only means something if the two worlds share something

Picture two paper maps of two different countries, with nothing in common — no shared city, no shared river, no shared grid. Lay one on top of the other. Is a given town on the first map "in the same place" as a town on the second? The question has no answer; you could slide one map over the other any way you like and nothing would change.

Two worlds that did not interact *at all* would be like those two maps. Saying they "share the same space" would be words without content. For "the same place" to mean anything, there has to be at least one thing that appears on both maps.

*How sure are we?* Very. This is a matter of logic about how the equations are built, not a guess.

### 2. The thing they share is gravity — and gravity cannot be switched off

The simplest shared thing is gravity. And physics gives a firm rule here: gravity pulls on *everything* that has energy, with the same strength. There is no known consistent way to have matter that gravity ignores. So a hidden world in our space would weigh something. Its planets and stars would tug on ours.

This is not an exotic idea: astronomers already know that most of the matter in the universe is invisible "dark matter" that we detect only through its gravity. A hidden world would simply be a kind of dark matter.

But it has a sharp consequence. Scientists know the mass of the Earth very precisely from how satellites and the Moon orbit it, and they know how much rock and iron is inside from studying earthquake waves. The two agree. That leaves room for at most about **0.4 %** of extra, invisible mass hiding inside the Earth.

So a twin planet sitting in the same place as the Earth cannot be anything like the Earth. If it were as big, it would have to be about as light as **styrofoam** on average — and its inhabitants would be held to their ground not by their own planet's gravity but by ours. To their scientists, *we* would be a huge mysterious invisible mass under their feet.

*How sure are we?* The 0.4 % figure is from a published study (2000). The rule that gravity is universal is as well established as anything in physics. There is one escape route — theories with two kinds of gravity at once — which we identified but did not explore.

### 3. Why we would not ordinarily see or feel the other world

Everything you see, hear and touch comes down to electric and magnetic forces between atoms. If the other world's matter does not respond to *our* electric force, and ours does not respond to theirs, then their light passes through us, their walls pass through our walls, and neither side notices. Physicists call this a "hidden sector" or "mirror matter". It is a respectable, well-studied idea. **This part of the hypothesis is not new**, and we do not claim it as ours.

### 4. A switch cannot be added by hand

To get a brief touch, something has to turn an interaction on for a moment and then off again. The tempting move is to just write that into the equations: "the two worlds interact here, for this long."

That breaks the bookkeeping. Momentum — roughly, "amount of motion" — is never created or destroyed; if something gets a push, something else gets an equal and opposite push. We showed, and confirmed by computer simulation, that a switch imposed from outside hands out pushes with nothing pushing back. The only way to fix it is for the switch itself to be a physical thing that can carry energy and motion. We call it the **gate**: an extra invisible field (a "field" is something that has a value at every point in space, like temperature in a room) that controls whether the two worlds can feel each other at each place.

*How sure are we?* Very. This is the standard conservation law, and the simulation agreed with the formula to better than one part in a thousand.

### 5. How strong would the push have to be?

A touch you can clearly feel is roughly the weight of a **paperclip resting on your fingertip**. That is a tiny force by everyday standards — but it turns out to be enormous by the standards of hidden-world physics.

Physicists know of only a few ways a hidden world could be faintly linked to ours ("portals"). We worked out the most generous case for each — every single atom in both bodies cooperating perfectly:

- A link as strong as the **weak nuclear force** (the feeble force behind some kinds of radioactivity) would give a push about **20,000 times too small** to feel.
- A link through the **Higgs particle** would be hundreds of millions of times too small.
- A link through a **faint mixing of their light with ours** gives, to first approximation, *no push at all* between ordinary objects. Atoms are electrically neutral — equal amounts of plus and minus — and we proved that the pushes and pulls cancel exactly. (An older published estimate says otherwise; we think it left out half of the cancellation, but we could be wrong about that and it needs checking by someone else.)

So with any known kind of link, the hand passes through you and you feel nothing. Feeling it requires a new force, present only during the touch, about twenty thousand times stronger than the weak force.

*How sure are we?* Fairly, with a caveat: "how hard is the faintest touch" is our estimate, good to maybe a factor of ten. Even being generous, the known links fall short by a factor of hundreds. (Interestingly, the ear is vastly more sensitive than the skin. A hidden object might be *heard* long before it could be felt.)

### 6. A push would come with heat

Here is something we did not expect. Any link strong enough to push would also let the atoms of the two bodies knock energy back and forth. We estimate that a hidden hand overlapping yours would exchange heat with your hand throughout its whole volume, coming to the same temperature within a couple of seconds at most, possibly within a thousandth of a second. A touch from the other world would feel **warm or cold all the way through**, not just like pressure on the skin.

It also means the link cannot be on all the time: two worlds in permanent contact that strong would long ago have reached the same temperature everywhere.

### 7. The best mechanism we could build

We looked for the least artificial switch. The best candidate works like this.

Think of an automatic door with a pressure plate that only responds when **two people stand on it together**. One alone does nothing. In our model the gate field stays completely shut — exactly shut, not just nearly — unless dense matter from *both* worlds is in the same spot, over a region bigger than a certain minimum size. A hidden hand moving through our air does nothing, because air is too thin. A hidden hand overlapping a human body opens the gate, but only in the region where they overlap.

What happens then, according to the equations:

- The two bodies **attract** each other gently, like a weak magnet. It is a tug, not a push. We proved it can only be a tug in this kind of model.
- The force has a definite maximum strength that we can calculate.
- When the bodies move apart, the gate shuts and the interaction is gone. No lasting connection.
- The event sends out a faint ripple in the gate field — a very small fraction of an already small energy.
- Energy and momentum balance exactly. Nothing travels faster than light.

We simulated the whole sequence on a computer — approach, overlap, hold, withdraw — and the simulation matched the formulas to five significant figures. In that limited sense, **the thought experiment can be realised mathematically.**

*What the simulations do and do not show.* They show the equations do what we say. They are not experiments on nature. A simulation of a model can never show that the model is true.

### 8. Why it probably is not how the world works

Being honest about the problems matters more than the model. There are five serious ones.

**The balancing act.** In quantum physics, fields are never perfectly still; they jitter. For our gate, that jitter acts like a foot permanently wedged in the door. To keep the door properly shut, a number in the equations would have to be adjusted to cancel the jitter to about one part in a trillion, with no reason for it to come out that way. Physicists treat that kind of coincidence as a strong sign that a model is wrong. There is a version of the gate without this problem, but it is not really a switch — just a force that is extremely weak unless both bodies are dense.

**The missing particles.** A force that strong, acting on the particles inside atoms, would have to be carried by new, light particles. Experiments have searched hard for such things. We did not manage to show that any version of our model slips through those searches, and it may well not.

**Sticky nuclei.** The attraction that produces the gentle tug on the large scale becomes a strong pull on the tiny scale of atomic nuclei. Nuclei from the two worlds could pass right through each other, and the pull might be strong enough to bind them together. If that happened, the two bodies would weld themselves together and release a great deal of energy. We found this problem late, while trying to attack our own work, and we could not resolve it.

**The floor problem.** A gate that opens when a hand overlaps a hand would open even more readily when a hand overlaps a rock, a wall or a floor. If people of another world really lived alongside us, we would constantly feel their buildings and they would feel ours. We do not. So the model only makes sense if solid matter from the other world is almost never here — which contradicts the picture of another person standing in "the corresponding room".

**No reason for them to be here.** Nothing in the model says how often such an encounter should happen, or why it ever would. That also means no amount of *not* being touched could prove the idea wrong, which is a real weakness for a scientific proposal.

## What we learned

**Solid (we would defend these):**
- Two worlds that never interact in any way are not meaningfully "in the same place".
- If they share gravity, the other world cannot be a twin Earth.
- A switch between worlds cannot be imposed from outside; it must be a physical thing.
- Feeling a hidden object needs a link about 20,000 times stronger than the known kinds could supply.
- A link that strong would carry heat as well as force.
- There is a general trade-off: a strong force *between* the worlds, carried the usual way, forces an even stronger force *within* one of them. Avoiding that requires the unusual "both together" kind of link our model uses.

**Speculative (interesting, unproven):**
- The "two on the pressure plate" gate. It works as mathematics. Whether anything like it exists is unknown, and there are good reasons to doubt it.
- Our disagreement with one older published estimate.
- An idea, not worked out, that extra dimensions of space might remove the "balancing act" problem.

**Not claimed:**
- That other worlds exist.
- That anyone has ever been touched by one. Unexplained sensations have ordinary explanations — nerves, muscles, air currents, attention — and are not evidence for any of this.
- That this is a discovery. Much of it is a new arrangement of existing ideas, checked carefully.

## Is the original thought experiment physically plausible?

In the weakest sense, yes: we found no law of physics that flatly forbids it, and we wrote down equations in which it happens.

In any stronger sense, no. It requires an unknown force of a strength that experiments would probably have noticed; a hiding mechanism that is either a one-in-a-trillion coincidence or not a real switch; a solution to the sticky-nuclei problem that we do not have; and a visitor who has no reason to be there. Each of those is a long shot, and all of them would have to hold together.

If it *did* happen the way the model says, it would not feel like a hand. It would feel like a brief, soft tug on a patch of skin, starting abruptly, accompanied by warmth or chill deep in the tissue, with nothing to see.

## How far to trust this report

- The mathematics is simple by professional standards and was checked three ways: by hand, by a computer-algebra program, and by simulation. All checks pass, and the code is included so anyone can rerun it.
- The scientific references were verified to exist (82 of them, automatically), and the key numbers were checked against the papers' own summaries.
- Three of our own early claims turned out to be wrong when tested, and were corrected. They are listed in the research journal.
- No human physicist has reviewed any of it. The "adversarial review" in this project is the same AI arguing against itself — useful, but not a substitute.

## Glossary

- **Sector / hidden sector** — a complete set of particles and forces. Ours is one; a hidden sector is another that does not respond to our electric and nuclear forces.
- **Mirror matter** — the best-known hidden-sector idea: a copy of our particles that keeps to itself.
- **Portal** — any faint link between a hidden sector and ours.
- **Field** — something with a value at every point in space, like temperature or wind.
- **Gate** — in this project, an extra field whose value decides whether the two worlds can interact at a given place.
- **Momentum** — amount of motion; total momentum never changes.
- **Weak force** — one of the four known basic forces; very feeble at everyday distances.
- **Dark matter** — invisible matter known only through its gravity; a hidden world would be a kind of it.
- **Fine-tuning** — when a model only works if a number is adjusted to extreme precision for no reason.
